# Shell-back

A massive terrestrial gastropod native to Aurelion's eastern slope — the same
scarp-and-scrub ecoregion that produces the Scarp Adder and the Talus Hound.
Where those two are predators, the Shell-back is a slow, armored forager,
built for load-bearing endurance and near-total invulnerability rather than
speed. It is the only large animal on the river route that never needs to
stop, never needs to be defended, and never loses its footing — which is the
specific reason nomadic caravans keep them instead of oxen or mules.

## Appearance

- A low, wide, heavy-bodied creature — no long neck, no legs, nothing about
  it reads as graceful. At rest it is roughly the footprint of a small cart
  and chest-height on an average man at the peak of the shell. Everything
  about its design language is load-bearing, not elegant
- The shell is thick, ridged, and colored to match the scarp itself — banded
  grey and rust-ochre in layered striations, the same split-shale look as
  the Scarp Adder's scales. A resting Shell-back is genuinely easy to
  mistake for an outcropping of stone. The ridges aren't smooth spiral
  whorls; they're broad, flattened, almost architectural plates, more like
  sedimentary terracing than a seashell
- Where the aperture would sit on an ordinary gastropod, a Shell-back has a
  hardened operculum — a natural plate the animal can seal shut over the
  cargo cavity. Domesticated Shell-backs are typically fitted with an iron
  collar and hinged latch around this natural seal, so it reads less like an
  animal orifice and more like a ship's hold hatch
- The muscular foot is thick, grey-brown, deeply creased, and permanently
  damp — not slimy so much as tanned-leather-left-out-in-the-rain. Its
  faint mineral sheen comes from the same hardening mucus the creature uses
  to grip stone and seal its hatch, and years of scree crossings leave tiny
  embedded grit flecks worked into the skin
- The head is blunt and low, with short, thick sensory stalks rather than
  the long delicate ones of a common snail — stubby and stone-knuckled,
  giving it a watchful, unbothered expression rather than a comic one
- Like most terrestrial gastropods, Shell-backs are hermaphroditic. Nomad
  handlers still use "he" or "she" for the ones they've worked with for
  years, out of habit rather than because it reflects anything meaningful
  to the animal itself

## Habitat & Range

Native to the same transitional scarp and scrub country as the Scarp Adder
and Talus Hound — the strip of rocky, river-fed land between Grondia's
eastern face and the river valley (see
`docs/lore/environments/eastern-approach/eastern-approach-profile.md`). Wild
Shell-backs are solitary foragers, grazing scrub and river-bottom vegetation
at their own pace and retreating into their shells at the first sign of a
threat. They are rarely seen moving quickly, rarely seen moving in groups,
and — because of how effective their defense is — rarely seen dead of
anything but old age.

## Behavior

- Continuous forager; grazes almost constantly while moving, which means it
  never needs a dedicated feeding stop the way a horse or ox does
- Tireless by nature — a wild Shell-back's day-night cycle shows no real
  rest period beyond brief stillness; it simply keeps moving, slowly, for as
  long as the terrain allows
- Deliberate, methodical foot placement; tests new ground — loose scree, a
  riverbed, an uncertain ledge — before committing its full weight, which is
  part of why it essentially never loses footing
- When startled, vents a short hiss of air from within the shell cavity — a
  slight pressure-release rather than a voice, the closest thing the
  species has to a vocalization
- Under sustained threat, withdraws foot and head fully into the shell and
  seals the operculum. What's left is, functionally, a boulder

## Special Traits

- **Armored Withdrawal**: Full retreat into the shell with the operculum
  sealed leaves no exposed target — no throat, no legs, no back to flank.
  Predators native to this ecoregion have no established way to profitably
  engage a sealed Shell-back (see "Natural Defenses" below)
- **Grip-Optimized Foot**: The same muscular foot and mineral-hardening
  mucus that make it slow also make it nearly impossible to dislodge from
  wet stone, loose scree, or a riverbed — the tradeoff that makes it worth
  keeping despite the pace
- **Submersible Crossing**: A Shell-back doesn't swim a river — it seals its
  hatch and simply keeps crawling along the riverbed, fully submerged. The
  same operculum seal that keeps cargo dry means the crossing is a non-event
  for the animal, where it's the hardest obstacle on the route for every
  other pack animal
- **Continuous Motion**: Never requires scheduled rest, feeding stops, or
  recovery time — the trait that offsets its slow pace over the length of a
  real journey (see `docs/lore/creatures/shell-back-history.md`)
- **Natural Defenses**: See below

## Natural Defenses

The shell is the whole defense — no claw, no venom, no speed. Threatened,
a Shell-back draws its foot and head fully into the shell and seals the
hatch. The plates are thick enough that sustained clawing or gnawing wears
down the attacker long before the shell gives.

Both predators established in this ecoregion already have documented
behavior that makes a sealed Shell-back a poor target on their own terms:

- **Talus Hounds** (see `docs/lore/enemies/talus_hound.md`) hunt through
  flanking and hit-and-run — tactics built on exploiting panic, isolation,
  and an angle to attack from. A sealed Shell-back doesn't panic, doesn't
  run, and offers no angle. A pack gets nothing to coordinate against, and
  experienced packs are known to disengage quickly rather than waste hours
  on a target that can't be cracked
- **Scarp Adders** (see `docs/lore/enemies/scarp_adder.md`) are already
  documented as intelligent and cautious, avoiding unnecessary combat with
  creatures much larger than themselves — a sealed Shell-back offers no
  exposed warm-blooded strike point for their heat-sensing pits to key on,
  and far too much bulk to provoke

Nothing native to this stretch of the eastern slope meaningfully preys on
an adult Shell-back. It isn't that it wins fights — there's no fight to be
had.
